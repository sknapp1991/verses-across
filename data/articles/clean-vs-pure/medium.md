## Introduction

The gap between the food laws of the Hebrew Scriptures and the freedom most Christians practice today is one of the longest-running debates in biblical theology. At first sight the question of whether a believer may eat pork looks simple: text against context, or law against grace. Look closer and it rests on careful distinctions of language, on how ancient people classified the world, and on how the covenants relate to each other.

This essay separates four words that English tends to blur (clean, pure, dirty and impure), explains the difference between ritual impurity and forbidden food, asks why the pig in particular stood out, and then sets out the strongest case on each side: the Sabbatarian and Hebrew Roots case that the law still stands, and the case for freedom under the New Covenant held by most Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and Protestant Christians.

## Four words that are not the same

### Clean (Hebrew *tahor*, Greek *katharos*)

In the Bible, "clean" is not about hygiene. *Tahor* describes a thing in its normal, whole state, in line with the order of creation and free from anything that would disqualify it. It is the ordinary condition needed to take part in everyday life and in worship. In the New Testament, *katharos* keeps that sense and turns it inward too, toward a heart and conscience that are undivided: "Blessed are the pure in heart: for they shall see God." (Matthew 5:8)

### Holy (Hebrew *qadosh*, Greek *hagios*)

Where "clean" is the normal baseline, "holy" lifts a thing into consecration. Holiness is not just the absence of defilement; it is being set apart for God. Something can be clean without being holy, but nothing can be holy without first being clean. Holiness marked what could come near God's presence in the Tabernacle and the Temple. The Greek *hagios* carries the same idea: set apart from common use and given to God. (English Bibles often say "pure" for *katharos* and "holy" for *qadosh*; the essay's point is that these are two different steps.)

### Dirty (physical dirt)

Plain physical dirt belongs to ordinary life. Soil, dust, sweat and grime from honest work carry no ritual weight and are not sin. A farmer covered in field dust is dirty, yet ritually clean and free to take part in everyday covenant life. The law kept a firm line between hygiene and ritual status, a line that later disputes over hand-washing would test.

### Impure (Hebrew *tame*, Greek *akathartos*)

Impurity, *tame*, is a real status, and it can spread. It is not mere grime; it marks contact with the forces that break the order of life, above all death, serious skin disease and bodily discharges. The Greek *akathartos* carries the same idea, and the Gospels use it for "unclean spirits" too. Impurity and holiness cannot mix: an impure person who entered the holy place without the required purification was in grave danger (Leviticus 15:31).

### The four side by side

- **Clean:** the normal, whole state. Belongs to everyday and sacred life alike.
- **Holy:** set apart for God. Belongs to the sanctuary; reached by consecration, sacrifice and anointing.
- **Dirty:** physical grime. Belongs to ordinary life; dealt with by soap and water.
- **Impure:** a ritual status linked to death and decay. Dealt with by washing, sacrifice and waiting.

## Two different kinds of rule: ritual impurity and forbidden food

To untangle the pork debate, two separate kinds of law in Leviticus have to be kept apart: ritual impurity of the body, and the food laws. Running them together causes much confusion.

### Ritual impurity: temporary, and not a sin

Ritual impurity comes from meeting the unavoidable facts of mortal life. Touching a corpse, contracting a skin disease, or ordinary bodily discharges make a person *tame*. Catching ritual impurity is **not a sin**; it comes with being human. A man who buries his mother does right, and still becomes impure for a time. This kind of impurity is temporary (from one day to seven or more), passes by touch, and ends with washing in water and the setting of the sun, and for some conditions an offering too.

### Forbidden food: fixed, and a commandment

The food laws of Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14 work differently. The swine is named directly: "And the swine, though he divide the hoof, and be clovenfooted, yet he cheweth not the cud; he is unclean to you. Of their flesh shall ye not eat, and their carcase shall ye not touch; they are unclean to you." (Leviticus 11:7–8) Deuteronomy adds: "Thou shalt not eat any abominable thing." (Deuteronomy 14:3)

So the pig's status is fixed: it is not food for Israel. A living pig in a village does not spread impurity the way a corpse does (the law's touch rule concerns the dead carcass), but eating it is not a ritual accident to be washed away. It breaks a commandment of the covenant.

## Why the pig?

Why did the pig become the very picture of the unclean in biblical imagination? Some modern writers point to health, such as the parasite that causes trichinosis, or to pigs' scavenging habits. The anthropologist Mary Douglas, in her book *Purity and Danger* (1966), offered a different explanation.

Douglas argued that ancient purity rules rest on classification. God's holiness is reflected in wholeness, order and clear boundaries, so a clean animal is one that fits the pattern of its kind. For land animals the Bible gives two marks: "Whatsoever parteth the hoof, and is clovenfooted, and cheweth the cud, among the beasts, that shall ye eat." (Leviticus 11:3)

The pig meets exactly half the test. It has a fully split hoof, but it does not chew the cud. It shows the outward sign of a clean animal without the inner one: "matter out of place", in Douglas's phrase. On this reading the pig is left off the covenant table not because it is filthy, but because it blurs the lines of creation's order.

## The case that the ban still stands

Some Christians, such as Seventh-day Adventists, the Hebrew Roots movement and the Ethiopian Orthodox Church, hold that the ban on pork still binds believers today. Their case rests on God's unchanging character.

### Holiness does not change

The food laws are tied not to public health but to God's own nature: "For I am the LORD your God: ye shall therefore sanctify yourselves, and ye shall be holy; for I am holy" (Leviticus 11:44). Since God's holiness does not change, the boundaries that reflect it should not change either. With the Father of lights there "is no variableness, neither shadow of turning" (James 1:17); what was an abomination then, the argument goes, is one now.

### Older than Sinai

The distinction between clean and unclean animals did not begin at Mount Sinai. Noah, long before Israel existed, was told: "Of every clean beast thou shalt take to thee by sevens, the male and his female: and of beasts that are not clean by two". (Genesis 7:2) So the distinction looks like part of creation itself, not a temporary law for one nation.

### Answering the New Testament passages

- **Mark 7.** The dispute with the Pharisees was about the tradition of washing hands before eating, not about which animals are food. Jesus says that what enters a man "entereth not into his heart, but into the belly, and goeth out into the draught, purging all meats" (Mark 7:19). The King James wording describes digestion. Most modern translations, following the oldest Greek manuscripts, read it as the narrator's comment that Jesus "declared all foods clean"; this side holds that even so, the point concerned unwashed hands, not forbidden animals. And pork, this side argues, was never "food" to anyone in that conversation.
- **Acts 10.** Peter's vision of the sheet of animals is explained by Peter himself: "God hath shewed me that I should not call any man common or unclean." (Acts 10:28) The vision used his food scruples to teach him about people: Gentiles. To use it to change the menu ignores the meaning the apostle gave it.

### The prophets look forward

Isaiah's vision of the Lord coming in judgment includes this: "They that sanctify themselves, and purify themselves in the gardens behind one tree in the midst, eating swine’s flesh, and the abomination, and the mouse, shall be consumed together, saith the LORD." (Isaiah 66:17) If eating pork is still judged at the end of history, this side asks, how could it have been made clean in between?

## The case for freedom in Christ

Most Christians (Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and Protestant) read the New Testament as fulfilling the law rather than tearing it up. On this view, the cross, the resurrection and Pentecost transform the boundaries of the old covenant.

### The wall comes down

The food laws, on this reading, were a God-given teaching tool that marked Israel off from the nations around it, a wall of separation. Once Christ reconciled Jew and Gentile on the cross, the reality those signs pointed to had come, and the outward markers of separation were no longer needed.

### The temple moves inside

Under the old covenant, holiness was centered on a building, the Tabernacle or Temple, and outward impurity had to be kept away from it. Under the new, the believer is the temple: "know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you". (1 Corinthians 6:19) A temple within can only be defiled from within, by sin, malice and pride. As Jesus put it: "There is nothing from without a man, that entering into him can defile him: but the things which come out of him, those are they that defile the man." (Mark 7:15) Most modern translations add the Gospel writer's comment a few verses later: in saying this, Jesus "declared all foods clean" (Mark 7:19).

### What the apostles decided

- **The council in Jerusalem (Acts 15).** Asked what to require of Gentile believers, the apostles did not impose the whole law of Moses; of its food rules they kept only a few. They asked: "That ye abstain from meats offered to idols, and from blood, and from things strangled, and from fornication". (Acts 15:29)
- **Paul in Romans.** Paul, trained as a Pharisee, writes: "I know, and am persuaded by the Lord Jesus, that there is nothing unclean of itself: but to him that esteemeth any thing to be unclean, to him it is unclean." (Romans 14:14) And: "All things indeed are pure; but it is evil for that man who eateth with offence." (Romans 14:20) Food moves from cosmic law to conscience and love for one's neighbor.
- **Paul to Timothy.** Paul warns of teachers "commanding to abstain from meats, which God hath created to be received with thanksgiving of them which believe and know the truth. For every creature of God is good, and nothing to be refused, if it be received with thanksgiving". (1 Timothy 4:3–4)

## Beyond Christianity

The question is not only a Christian one. Jewish law has kept the ban on pork from the Torah to today, and the Qur'an forbids it too: "He hath forbidden you only carrion, and blood, and swineflesh, and that which hath been immolated to (the name of) any other than Allah." (Qur'an 2:173) For billions of people, not eating pork is a daily act of faithfulness.

## Conclusion

The difference between the two Christian views does not come from either side brushing Scripture aside. It comes from two different readings of the Bible's story. One sees **continuity**: the boundaries set at creation and at Sinai stay in place until the new heavens and the new earth. The other sees **a climax in Christ**: his work transforms sacred space, priesthood and the categories themselves.

For a Christian today, eating or abstaining is a way of standing in one of these two deep streams: honoring the unchanging order of creation's boundaries, or celebrating the freedom of the new covenant. Paul's own counsel covers both: "Let not him that eateth despise him that eateth not; and let not him which eateth not judge him that eateth" (Romans 14:3).
