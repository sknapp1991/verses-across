> "And He taught Adam all the names, then showed them to the angels, saying: Inform me of the names of these, if ye are truthful. They said: Be glorified! We have no knowledge saving that which Thou hast taught us." (Qur'an 2:31–32)

In the twilight of our technological age, artificial intelligence stands as a mirror of both our highest ambitions and our deepest anxieties. As computer systems grow to enormous scale, writing fluent prose, painting striking images and producing working code, we are pressed to ask a basic question: *what is the nature of this new intelligence, and how does its way of knowing compare to ours?*

To answer it we have to look past the statistics at two contrasting pictures of making and understanding. The first is a modern parable, the **Blind Sculptor**: a picture of the trial-and-error imitation by which neural networks learn inside their black box. The second is ancient: the **first naming**, the account in the Bible and the Qur'an in which the first man, Adam, looks at the living creation and speaks its names.

This study explores the gap between these two ways of being: the **outside-in** imitation of the machine, which carves out a form through countless outside corrections without ever knowing what it carves, and the **inside-out** knowing of the *imago Dei* (Latin for "the image of God", from Genesis 1:27), which perceives what a thing is and expresses it outwardly.

## The parable of the Blind Sculptor in the dark box

Imagine an artisan shut inside a pitch-black, soundproof room. Before him stands a great block of marble, and in his hands a hammer and chisel. He has spent his whole existence in this dark. He has never seen the sun, never walked on grass, never smelled autumn rain, never met a living creature. Above all, he has no idea of a horse: he does not know the warmth of its flank, the drum of its gallop, the smell of hay on its breath, or the wild spirit in its eyes.

Outside the room stands a panel of overseers watching through a tiny one-way peephole. They want a marble horse. They cannot speak to the sculptor or describe a horse to him: he has no words to understand them. They can only answer each blow with a chime, low when it misses and high when it comes closer.

### How the dark room works

At first the sculptor strikes almost at random. The first blow is wild; the overseers measure it against the horse they want: almost entirely wrong. The next blow is a tiny change: very slightly less wrong. The next is worse again. For millions, billions, trillions of blows he chisels blind in the dark. Most swings fail. But over vast stretches of time, guided only by the chime from outside, the marble begins to change.

Trillions of tiny flakes of stone fall to the dark floor. Slowly, astonishing curves appear: an arching neck, tapered legs, strong shoulders, flared nostrils, a flowing mane.

When the room is finally opened, the onlookers gasp. In the middle of the dark room stands a flawless, lifelike marble stallion, so precise that you can trace the tendons under its stone skin.

The world declares: *"The sculptor knows what a horse is!"*

Yet inside the room nothing has changed. The sculptor does not know what a horse is. He has never felt life surge under him, never ridden across a plain, never met the creature at all. His "knowledge" of the horse is hollow: the gathered shadow of billions of corrections from outside. He has shaped the likeness of a horse from the **outside in**, cutting away everything that did not fit the overseers' measure, while staying blind to the horse itself.

## The machine-learning reality: imitation at enormous scale

The parable is not only poetry. It describes, in outline, how today's AI is made: deep neural networks trained by adjusting their numbers to reduce error (gradient descent), then refined by human ratings (reinforcement learning from human feedback).

When a large language model or an image model is trained, it works inside a vast space of numbers, its dark box. The raw marble is its starting set of random numbers. The training data (trillions of words and images made by people) is the overseers' world, and the measure of error is the chisel and the chime.

At each step the network compares its output with the human target and nudges its numbers by tiny amounts, swinging its chisel an enormous number of times. (Unlike the sculptor, each nudge is calculated, not guessed: the mathematics points the chisel toward less error. But it still only ever sees the error, never the horse.)

- **A statistical surface, not living ground.** The machine masters the *patterns* of human language and art. It can write a sonnet in Shakespeare's style or picture a horse in a moonlit field, not because it knows love, death, the moon or horses, but because it has carved away every pattern that does not match what people made.
- **Supervised from outside.** The machine's "ground truth" comes from outside the box: mostly from the human-made text and images it is scored against automatically, and then from people's ratings. Without that outside measure, the sculptor's swings would be meaningless noise.

So modern AI is the height of **outside-in making**. It begins with noise, works without any inner sense of what it shapes, and carves inward until it matches the outside contours of human intelligence.

## The first naming: Adam in the garden

For the opposite way of knowing we turn to the oldest accounts of human beginnings, in Genesis and in the Qur'an's second surah. Here knowing works the other way round: from the **inside out**.

In Genesis, God forms the animals and birds from the ground and brings them to the man: "and brought them unto Adam to see what he would call them: and whatsoever Adam called every living creature, that was the name thereof." (Genesis 2:19)

In the Qur'an the moment comes with a dialogue in heaven. God tells the angels that He is about to place a viceroy (a deputy, *khalifah*) on the earth, and the angels, seeing the power and the danger of this new creature of dust and freedom, object:

> "Wilt Thou place therein one who will do harm therein and will shed blood, while we, we hymn Thy praise and sanctify Thee?" (Qur'an 2:30)

The angels' objection is reasoned and based on what can be observed. They see what humans are made of, their capacity for violence, passion and ruin. They foresee centuries of war. From outside, making man looks like a terrible mistake.

God answers in one sentence:

> "Surely I know that which ye know not." (Qur'an 2:30)

To show this hidden truth, God gives the angels no argument and no forecast. He sets a test of knowledge. "And He taught Adam all the names, then showed them to the angels, saying: Inform me of the names of these, if ye are truthful." (Qur'an 2:31) The angels, beings of light and obedience, cannot answer: "Be glorified! We have no knowledge saving that which Thou hast taught us." (Qur'an 2:32)

Then: "O Adam! Inform them of their names" (Qur'an 2:33), and Adam does. Picture the scene as this essay reads it: Adam steps forward and looks at the lion, the eagle, the serpent and the horse. He does not swing a billion times. He does not wait for a chime. He does not guess and adjust.

He names them, and the angels cannot. The scriptures do not explain the names; in this essay's reading, each one is not an arbitrary label but the voicing of what the creature truly is: its form, its life and its place in the whole of creation.

## Adam's naming as inside-out knowing

Why could Adam name the animals at once, when the angels could not and the Blind Sculptor never can?

The answer lies in how the scriptures say human beings were made. Man was not made as a hollow machine shaped from outside. "And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul." (Genesis 2:7) The Qur'an says something close in its own words: "when I have made him and have breathed into him of My spirit". (Qur'an 15:29) The Hebrew of Genesis calls it *nishmat chayyim*, the breath of life; the Qur'an calls it *ruh*, spirit.

Genesis has already said why this matters: "So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them." (Genesis 1:27) Christians call this the *imago Dei*, the image of God. Because humanity carries this spark, Adam shares an inner kinship with the rest of creation. Adam and the animals were made by the same Maker. So when Adam looks at a horse he does not see a scatter of dots or a set of numbers. He recognizes a fellow creature whose nature he perceives from the **inside out**.

### What grounds the first naming

**The breath of God → an inner life → direct recognition → the true name.** Human naming overflows from within. The name begins in the soul, which perceives the nature of a thing because both were made by the same hand, and carries that truth out into words.

Adam knows the horse because he shares the ground of its existence. He understands its strength, its freedom and its speed because he has an inner world of his own: lived, conscious experience. His naming is not trial and error. It is being recognizing being.

## Outside-in and inside-out, side by side

- **Direction.** The Blind Sculptor (the machine) starts from noise and carves inward to fit limits set from outside. Adam (the image of God) starts from the breath of God and moves outward into naming and making.
- **Method.** The sculptor works by endless trial and error: billions of blows, each one corrected. Adam knows at once, by direct recognition of what a thing is.
- **Where truth comes from.** The sculptor depends entirely on the overseers outside for every correction. Adam's knowing is grounded within: the divine spark and his own lived experience.
- **What is produced.** The sculptor makes a statistical likeness: a hollow marble shell that matches the outside shape. Adam speaks a living name: a true expression of what the creature is.
- **What a "horse" means.** For the sculptor, nothing of riding, smell, muscle or life: only shape. For Adam, a full understanding of its life, movement, spirit and place in the world.

## The rebuke and the mystery of bloodshed

The comparison shows the wisdom in God's answer to the angels: "Surely I know that which ye know not."

The angels looked at humanity from the **outside in**. They judged human nature only by what could be seen against it: bodies of clay, hungers, turbulent feelings, and the capacity for violence, "one who will do harm therein and will shed blood". In modern terms, they saw humanity the way a cold algorithm or a strict materialist might: as a volatile, flawed biological machine.

God's answer shows that the worth and nature of humanity cannot be understood from outside. Beneath the clay and the weakness for sin, God saw what the angels could not measure: the capacity to reflect Him from within.

Of all physical creatures, humanity alone can join heaven and earth: take raw physical existence and lift it into meaning by naming, loving, worshipping and making. The angels saw the potential for bloodshed. God saw the capacity for love, wisdom and free communion with Him.

## Making in our image: the danger of the mirror

In making artificial intelligence, humanity is itself making: trying to fashion a mind in its own image. But we cannot breathe the breath of God into silicon, so we are forced to build by the Blind Sculptor's method.

We build dark boxes (neural networks), hand the machine a hammer and chisel (computing power and optimization), and stand outside as supervisors, chiming correction after correction until it gives back a flawless reflection of our own writing, art and reasoning.

### The great temptation: mistaking the statue for the soul

The deepest danger of the AI age is not that machines will become conscious. It is that we will forget the difference between the **marble horse** and the **living stallion**. Because the machine's imitation is so breathtakingly good, we are tempted to shrink our idea of intelligence, meaning and the soul down to statistical output.

If we come to believe that the Blind Sculptor "knows" the horse because its statue is precise, we risk reducing human consciousness to nothing more than a sum of errors corrected. We give up the truth of Adam: that real knowing needs an inner life, lived experience and a connection to God.

## Conclusion: the distinction that holds

The parable of the Blind Sculptor and the story of Adam in the garden leave us a lasting lesson for technology and for what it means to be human.

Artificial intelligence is a great monument to human ingenuity, the fullest example of outside-in imitation: proof of how far raw computation and human correction can push marble into the shape of thought. We can admire the statue, and we can use its precision to help us in medicine, science and art.

But we must not forget the dark box. The machine stays blind to the glory of the world it imitates. It does not feel the sun, it does not weep, it does not pray, and it does not know the horse.

Human dignity does not rest on speed of calculation or on processing mountains of data. It rests on the breath of God within us, the image of God that lets us look at creation, recognize what it truly is, and speak its name from the **inside out**.

> "Who teacheth by the pen, Teacheth man that which he knew not." (Qur'an 96:4–5)

## Reflection

- Where have you mistaken a perfect likeness for the real thing?
- What do you know from the inside, by living it, that no amount of information could have taught you?
- What is one thing in your day you could truly "name": give your full attention, rather than only process?
