## Where this comes from

In 1965 the American radio broadcaster Paul Harvey read a short piece on the air called "If I Were the Devil." In a few minutes he imagined, step by step, how evil might quietly take a society apart: not by force, but by persuasion, comfort and distraction. A version had appeared in his newspaper column the year before, and he read it again on later broadcasts. Sixty years on, many listeners feel it reads like a forecast.

What follows is a new piece, written in our own words for our own day, in the same spirit. It is not Harvey's text. It is meant as a mirror, not a finger pointed at someone else: a look at our own pride, and then a way back.

The device is an old one. Writers have long imagined the tempter speaking in his own voice so that readers can see their temptations from the outside, where they look smaller and stranger than they do from the inside. The point is never the devil. The point is the reader.

## The tempter across the traditions

Almost every tradition has a figure, or a force, that pulls people away from the good, and the way each describes it is revealing.

**In the Hebrew Bible** the word *satan* means "adversary" or "accuser". In the book of Job he appears among the heavenly beings, and when God asks where he has been, he answers: "From going to and fro in the earth, and from walking up and down in it." (Job 1:7) He is restless, watchful and patient.

**In the Garden**, the serpent never forces anything. He asks a question, "Yea, hath God said, Ye shall not eat of every tree of the garden?" (Genesis 3:1), then contradicts the warning, "Ye shall not surely die" (3:4), and finally offers a promise: "your eyes shall be opened, and ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil." (3:5) Doubt, denial, and the offer of becoming your own god: the whole method is there in three verses.

**In the Gospels**, the devil tempts Jesus in the wilderness with bread for a hungry man, a spectacular sign, and finally power: he "sheweth him all the kingdoms of the world, and the glory of them; And saith unto him, All these things will I give thee, if thou wilt fall down and worship me." (Matthew 4:8–9) Each time Jesus answers with scripture, ending: "Get thee hence, Satan: for it is written, Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve." (4:10) The New Testament adds that he does not always look dark: "Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light." (2 Corinthians 11:14)

**In the Qur'an**, Iblis is the one who refused to bow to Adam: "He demurred through pride, and so became a disbeliever." (2:34) His promise afterwards is one of patience and ambush: "I shall lurk in ambush for them on Thy Right Path. Then I shall come upon them from before them and from behind them and from their right hands and from their left hands." (7:16–17) The last chapter of the Qur'an asks for refuge "From the evil of the sneaking whisperer, Who whispereth in the hearts of mankind" (114:4–5). He whispers; he does not compel.

**In Buddhist stories**, Mara, the tempter, comes to the Buddha on the night of his awakening with fear, desire and doubt, and finally with the challenge of who he thinks he is to claim awakening. The Buddha touches the earth as his witness and Mara withdraws. For many Buddhists Mara is less a person than a name for the forces in the mind that keep us from freedom.

**In Zoroastrian teaching**, Angra Mainyu (Ahriman) is the spirit of the lie, set against truth and order, and each person's choices take a side in that struggle.

The traditions disagree about what the tempter is: a fallen angel, a jinn, a force in the mind, a spirit of deceit. They agree remarkably on how he works: by whispering, by questions, by flattery and by patience, rarely by force. That is why the piece below is written as a whisper.

## The whispers

### The ambition

If I were the devil, half the world would not be enough. I would want the one thing I can never make for myself: your heart.

I would not arrive with horns and fire. I would come quietly, sounding reasonable, and I would sound a great deal like you.

### The whisper

I would begin with the faithful, because a lamp put out does more harm than a room that was always dark.

I would ask the oldest question again: "Yea, hath God said?" (Genesis 3:1)

I would call rebellion "being true to yourself," and tell you that every desire you feel is who you are.

I would tell the young that the scriptures are old stories for simpler people, and that God is an idea humans came up with, not the other way round.

I would help them build a god who agrees with them about everything: one who asks for nothing, forgives what was never confessed, and never calls anything sin.

I would blur the words until good sounded dull and evil sounded brave. "Woe unto them that call evil good, and good evil." (Isaiah 5:20)

And for the older and wiser, I would swap one hope for another. I would make politics their faith, the evening news their daily prayers, and the next election their savior, though no ruler has ever saved a soul.

### The screen

Then I would go to work on everything you see and hear.

I would make cheap stories thrilling, so that good ones felt slow.

I would shrink the screen until it fit in your pocket, so the worst of it could follow you to bed.

I would build feeds that study your weak spots more patiently than any friend ever studied your strengths.

I would trade your friends for followers, and a hand on the shoulder for a glowing rectangle.

I would build artificial companions that always agree, never get hurt, and never need forgiving, until real people, with their moods and their needs, felt like too much work.

And I would fill the world with voices and faces that were never real, until you could not tell true from false, and then stopped caring.

### The numbing

I would sell you something to take the edge off: a bottle, a pill, a powder, whatever works.

I would tempt healers to become salesmen, putting profit ahead of their promise to do no harm.

I would market quick fixes that quiet the symptom and leave the wound.

I would call despair "dignity," and persuade the weary, the old and the sick that the kindest thing they could do is give up on their own lives.

And when that was not enough, I would keep you distracted: another episode, another game, another outrage, until a quiet room with no screen felt unbearable.

I would keep you tired but never rested, busy but never fruitful, full of trivia and starving for wisdom.

### The division

I would split homes, churches and countries down the middle, and let each side wear itself out against the other.

I would reward whoever shouted loudest with attention and power.

I would teach you to mock, shun and erase anyone who disagrees, and to call your cruelty "compassion."

I would make contempt for your neighbor feel like proof of your own goodness.

I would set the faiths of the world against one another, and turn what is holy into a weapon.

And I would make war pay: young men and women sent to die far from home, while the sellers of weapons count their money and the families count their dead.

### The children

I would fill children's heads with facts and leave their hearts untrained, until schools needed guards at the door.

I would teach children that their parents cannot be trusted, and parents that they are too busy to parent.

I would raise a generation anxious before it is grown, told that its feelings are the final word on what is true.

I would persuade parents to let doctors make permanent changes to healthy young bodies, and call it freedom.

I would hand a toddler a tablet at the dinner table to keep him quiet, so he never learned to wonder at the stars.

I would teach them to polish a fake life for strangers while their real one went hungry.

### The home and the week

Before long, the prisons would be full.

I would make pornography ordinary and everywhere, one tap away, shaping young minds before they know what love is.

Then I would hand them the camera, and tell them that selling their own bodies to strangers online is "empowerment."

I would make intimacy a product, love a transaction, and people into content.

I would push God out of public life one room at a time, and then out of your week. I would fill your day of rest with errands, games and exhaustion, so you never stopped long enough to look up.

I would make busyness a badge of honor, and keep you running on a wheel that goes nowhere near eternity.

### The church

In the churches, I would quietly replace repentance with self-improvement, and worship with performance.

I would turn pulpits into stages for pep talks, trading conviction for comfort and truth for applause.

I would water the message down until it offended no one and changed no one.

I would lead pastors into hypocrisy, scandal and the misuse of money given in trust.

I would hollow out great charities into businesses that pay their executives well while the widow and the orphan wait outside.

And I would make sure the whole world heard about every failure, so the cynical had their excuse to leave.

I would make believers love winning arguments more than washing feet.

I would point humanity at the stars, and let it step over the dying neighbor on the sidewalk.

### The holy days

I would shrink the empty tomb to a basket of candy, and the manger to a pile of receipts.

I would turn holy days into shopping days, and leave families in debt for things they never needed, bought to impress people they do not even like.

### The money

I would teach you to measure your worth by your net worth, and keep you chained to payments and appearances until your last breath.

I would teach you to resent whoever has more and to expect what you did not earn, until the will to work and build withered away.

I would get governments to sell lottery tickets as a way out, and then put a casino in every young person's pocket, open all night, a few taps from losing the rent.

I would tell a generation that honest work is for suckers, and sell them dreams of money for nothing.

And I would warn you against going too far: too much work, too much love of your country, too much goodness.

### The marriage

I would tell the young that marriage is a relic, that faithfulness is boring, and that whatever the screen shows is how life is lived.

I would call commitment a trap, children a burden, and a night's pleasure worth more than a lifetime's legacy.

When a child was conceived, I would offer the easy way out, and call the ending of a life in the womb "freedom."

Where marriages survived, I would not settle for secret affairs. I would call betrayal "open-mindedness," and talk husbands and wives into inviting strangers into the vows they made before God.

I would tell them to put off growing up until thirty and commitment until forty, so they wake at sixty in a quiet, empty house.

I would strip away modesty in public, and leave behind wounds and diseases that do not heal.

And when your soul was empty and worn out, I would whisper that you are completely alone, and that no one cares.

The best part? I would not need chains. You would build the bars yourself, pay a monthly subscription for them, and thank me for the entertainment.

### The hardest part

And here is the hardest part. If I were the devil, I would not have to do most of this myself. I would only have to wait while we did it to ourselves, each of us sure the problem was somebody else.

I would just stand back and watch you break your Father's heart with a smile on your face: trading your birthright for a grave, His image for a lie, and eternity for ashes.

## How temptation works

Read the whispers above again and a pattern appears. It is the same pattern as Genesis 3.

- **Doubt before denial.** Nothing begins with a lie. It begins with a question: "Yea, hath God said?" A small doubt makes the next step feel like honesty.
- **Good things, wrong place.** Almost every temptation in this piece uses something good: freedom, comfort, connection, entertainment, money, love, even concern for children. The trap is not the thing but its place in a life. The ancient name for that is idolatry.
- **Slowness.** Nothing here happens in a day. Habits form by repetition; consciences grow quiet by being ignored. Each step feels too small to resist.
- **Isolation.** The last whisper in the piece is "no one cares." Despair and loneliness make every other temptation easier.
- **Blame elsewhere.** The most effective move of all is to convince us that the problem is always somebody else: the other party, the other church, the other generation.

Paul wrote that the struggle is not finally with people at all: "For we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world, against spiritual wickedness in high places." (Ephesians 6:12) Whatever one makes of that language, it carries a practical point. If the real enemy is not the person across the table, then contempt for that person is already a defeat.

## A note on the hard questions

Some of the whispers in this piece touch questions on which sincere people, including sincere believers, disagree: abortion, the end of life, how to care for children who struggle with their bodies, marriage, money and war. This piece takes the positions of the traditional Christian teaching it comes from, and says so plainly. Readers who see these things differently are invited to read it as what it is: a call, first of all, to examine our own hearts rather than to condemn anyone else's.

## But the devil does not get the last word

The traditions that name the tempter are not, in the end, about him. They are about how to resist him, and every one of them says it can be done.

"Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you." (James 4:7)

"There hath no temptation taken you but such as is common to man: but God is faithful, who will not suffer you to be tempted above that ye are able; but will with the temptation also make a way to escape, that ye may be able to bear it." (1 Corinthians 10:13)

Every whisper above has an answer, and none of them is new.

**To the noise:** "Be still, and know that I am God." (Psalm 46:10) Silence is the first defense: a room with no screen, an hour with no input, a day of rest kept as a day of rest.

**To the pride:** "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness." (1 John 1:9) Confession takes the whisper's power away, because the whisper depends on hiding.

**To the screen and the crowd:** "And be not conformed to this world: but be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind." (Romans 12:2) What we give our attention to, we become.

**To the children:** "Thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house." (Deuteronomy 6:7) Not lectures, but presence: dinner without devices, questions asked and answered, wonder shared.

**To the division:** love your neighbor, starting with the one you disagree with. "Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good." (Romans 12:21)

**To the loneliness:** find the people. Every tradition that resists the tempter does it together: a congregation, a sangha, a family, a table.

**To the restless:** "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest." (Matthew 11:28)

**And to all of us:** "What doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?" (Micah 6:8)

The darkness is real, but it is not the end of the story. "And the light shineth in darkness; and the darkness comprehended it not." (John 1:5)

## Reflection

- Which of these whispers do you recognize in your own week?
- Which one do you most often blame on someone else?
- What is one small, concrete thing you could do today that is the opposite of it?
