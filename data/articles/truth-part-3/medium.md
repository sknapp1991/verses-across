*To say that an ultimate reality exists is to trap it inside a yes-or-no gate; to say that it does not exist is to build a monument to its absence. Truth is neither the switch nor the current flowing through it: it is the circuit's capacity to hold both light and dark without being consumed by either.*

This part follows a Buddhist line of thought in its own voice and sets it beside the opening of Genesis. It is one tradition's way of seeing, offered for reflection, not the last word.

## The trap of the yes-or-no gate

Much Western inquiry is binary. A question comes to the mind like a computer's yes-or-no test, waiting for a 1 (true, being, yes) or a 0 (false, non-being, no). Asked whether a Creator God exists, Western theology answers 1: an uncaused First Cause. Classical Western atheism answers 0: a mechanical universe without divine purpose.

Yet both answers share a hidden premise: **both assume that "Creator God" is the kind of thing a yes-or-no gate can evaluate.**

When Indian Buddhist thought meets this binary, it does not simply vote 0. Its tools are the *catuskoti*, the "four corners" (is, is not, both, neither), and the Buddha's *avyakata*, the "undeclared" questions he refused to answer either way. To say "Buddhism rejects a creator god" and stop there misreads a diagnosis as a plain denial. Rejecting 1 is not automatically choosing 0; choosing 0 is still playing inside the same box. Rejection in its deepest sense is taking the gate itself apart.

### First picture: the computer and the candle flame

Imagine a machine built to evaluate logic gates. You enter the statement: *"The flame of the blown-out candle is hiding under the table."* The machine tries to answer 1 or 0. If it says 0, it implies the flame is hiding somewhere else, say in the closet. If it says 1, the flame is under the table. But the flame is neither: it has gone back into the heat and air it came from. To answer truly, the operator has to break the switch.

The Buddha used this very image. Asked where an enlightened one goes after death, he asked in return which direction a fire goes when it goes out: north, south, east or west? The question, he said, does not fit the case.

This is not clever evasion; it is precision. Putting a question that is not yes-or-no to a yes-or-no test produces a category mistake. To question every answer in this way is not to stay silent from ignorance. It is to show that every formal statement (it is, it is not, both, neither) is language laid over a reality that does not fit inside it.

## No first moment: the self-sustaining loop

The mind demands a starting point. Linear thinking says that if there is a chain A → B → C, something must have started the push. So when a philosophy says "the universe has no first moment and no outside architect," we struggle to picture it.

But "no first moment" can be pictured as **a beginningless, self-sustaining loop**, and "no outside architect" as **a world that sustains itself from within.**

Take the old puzzle of the seed and the tree. Which came first? Say the seed, and you need a tree to bear it. Say the tree, and you need a seed to sprout it. Linear logic breaks down and calls for an outside actor, a divine hand, to drop the first seed.

### The web of seed, tree and world

The seed is not an isolated object that makes a tree. Seed, soil, water, warmth, sunlight, air and the genetic pattern form a **web of mutual dependence**. The seed does not come before the tree in any absolute sense; seeding and treeing are phases of one unbroken loop. Without soil and sun a seed is not a seed, just matter; without the seed the soil's richness stays unexpressed. They arise together: what the Buddha called *paticca-samuppada*, dependent origination.

From here the Buddhist argument goes further. If something stood wholly outside the web of cause and effect (unconditioned, unchanging, eternal), it would have no point of contact with the conditioned world. To act on the world is to enter a relationship; to enter a relationship is to be affected; to be affected is to change. So, the argument runs, a creator who acts is already part of the causal web, and cannot be its unconditioned outside source.

## Genesis and the void

Here Buddhist thought and the Bible's first page come surprisingly close. Genesis opens:

> "In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth. And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep." (Genesis 1:1–2)

The Hebrew for "without form, and void" is *tohu va-vohu*. Popular readings take "void" as absolute nothing, a literal 0. But many interpreters, ancient and modern, read it differently: **the void is not emptiness as absence, but emptiness as unformed potential.** The emptiness of everything is the possibility of anything.

In that void, you could say, interaction both was and was not. It was not, because nothing yet stood apart as opposites: light and dark, land and sea. It was, because the full capacity for everything to come was already present, unmanifest. And Genesis does not leave the deep alone: "And the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters." (Genesis 1:2)

### Second picture: the blank canvas

Think of a white canvas before the painter touches it. Is it empty or full? It holds no painted scene (0, as to form), yet it holds every painting that could ever be made (1, as to potential). The void is not a barren desert. Physicists say something similar of the vacuum: even "empty" space is never perfectly still, but restless with possibility.

On this reading, the theological puzzle loosens: **the ultimate cannot be trapped in fixed attributes.** Call God purely permanent, and God seems lifeless, incapable of creating. Call God purely changing, and God seems chaotic, without identity. Call God complete, and creation seems unnecessary; incomplete, and God seems flawed. The absolute will not be pinned to permanent or impermanent, changing or unchanging, complete or incomplete. Read this way, the void of Genesis is where all these pairs fold back into their unmanifest source.

## Four classical critiques, one insight

Indian Buddhist philosophers examined the idea of a single outside creator (*Ishvara*) with great care. Their arguments are usually studied one by one, but they point the same way:

- **Nagarjuna (2nd century): emptiness.** Nothing has *svabhava*, independent self-existence. A creator that existed independently could have no relation to creation; one that depended on creation to be "creator" would not be independent.
- **Vasubandhu (4th century): time.** If an all-powerful creator were the sole cause of the world, everything would have to arise at once. If things arise one after another, the creator depends on other conditions (time, materials, desire), and so is not the sole cause.
- **Dharmakirti (7th century): causation.** A cause that never changes cannot sometimes produce effects and sometimes not; a creator that acts at one time and not another must change, which undoes the idea of an unchanging ultimate.
- **Shantarakshita (8th century): permanence.** In his *Tattvasangraha* he pressed the point that a permanent, unchanging cause could never produce effects one after another. (The related "who designed the designer?" regress is best known from the Scottish philosopher David Hume.)

**The thread that joins them,** as this tradition sees it: not four arguments but one diagnosis, aimed at the same trick of language: the belief that a subject can exist without an object, a cause without an effect, or an unconditioned actor act within a conditioned world.

## Living beyond the binary

What, then, is truth?

On this view, truth is not a statement to be confirmed with a 1 or refuted with a 0. It is not a fixed doctrine held in the mind, nor a void where awareness disappears. Truth is the direct recognition of the beginningless, self-sustaining web of existence.

Letting go of an outside architect, the Buddhist says, does not drop us into a cold, empty cosmos. We find instead that **the whole cosmos is its own living, self-arising architecture.** Every point in the web, every star, every seed, every human thought, is at once maker and made, cause and effect, observer and observed.

### Third picture: Indra's net

In the heaven of the god Indra hangs a vast net stretching endlessly in every direction. At every knot hangs a single polished jewel, and in each jewel every other jewel is reflected, reflections within reflections without end. No jewel is the first; no jewel is the master. Each holds all the others, and all hold each.

To live in truth, on this reading, is to step off the seesaw of yes and no. It is to see that the void of Genesis, the silence of the Buddha and the living web of cause and effect need not be competing doctrines, but may be views of one reality, seen when the mind finally stops asking a world that is not yes-or-no to fit inside a yes-or-no box.
